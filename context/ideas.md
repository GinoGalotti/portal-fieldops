### The Soul Fraud Case
**Status:** DISCUSSED
**Type:** One-off / April Fools candidate / Season 7–8 seed
**Premise:** A pathetic man is offered a demon deal — six months of whatever he wants in exchange for his soul. He says no. The demon signs the contract anyway and commits soul fraud. The man is now experiencing extraordinary good luck (birds land on his shoulder, he keeps winning scratch cards, his commute is inexplicably smooth) while desperately attempting to reach other demons to nullify the agreement. PORTAL gets involved when the luck starts affecting people around him in ways that suggest a supernatural mechanism.
**Tone:** Explicitly comic. Bureaucratic supernatural comedy — the demon equivalent of a terms-of-service violation. The man is not a villain. The demon is not malevolent, just opportunistic and poorly supervised.
**Possible mechanics:** The hunters have to navigate demonic bureaucracy to nullify the contract. There is a complaints process. It takes forever. Meanwhile the client's luck is escalating toward something genuinely dangerous.
**Timing:** Too cartoonish for the current arc but perfect as a standalone one-off. Could be themed as an April Fools session. Could seed something larger in a later season if the demonic bureaucracy turns out to be interesting.
**Open questions:** Is demonic contract law a whole thing in this world? If so, what else does it cover? Are there other cases PORTAL has quietly filed as "contractual anomaly" and never pursued?

### Rook's Withdrawal Notebook
**Status:** DISCUSSED
**What it is:** Rook keeps a notebook during Substance Θ withdrawal periods — 12–18 hours of unreliable perception where she writes things down. She has never shown it to anyone. The notebook surfaces late campaign.
**The question:** What does she write during withdrawal? Two possibilities that aren't mutually exclusive — (A) the most important data in the campaign: observations she can only access in the liminal state between Θ and baseline perception, things she sees that she can't see straight. (B) the most disturbing: what Rook is like when she isn't performing competence, what she actually thinks about what MESA does, what she has decided to live with and what she hasn't.
**When to deploy:** Late Arc 1 or Arc 2. The notebook arriving in play should feel inevitable. It could be a gift, a theft, or something she chooses to share.

### Demonic Contract Law
**Status:** DISCUSSED
**What it is:** If the Soul Fraud case gets developed, it implies that demonic contracts are a real legal framework in this world — with terms, violations, appeals processes, and presumably a body that adjudicates disputes. PORTAL has probably encountered the edges of this before without recognising it.
**Possible angle:** John Johnson (the Flake) is the natural character to become obsessed with this. He would immediately connect it to everything else. He would be wrong about most of the connections and right about one of them.
**Tone:** Best kept light and bureaucratic — the horror is that it's mundane, not that it's terrifying.

### The Soul Fraud — Azrameth / Expanded Mechanics
**Status:** DISCUSSED
**Type:** Comic one-off. No BIM connection. Standalone.
**The demon:** Azrameth, designation "Junior Acquisitions, Northeast Sector." About five foot six, polo shirt, sweaty, clipboard. Desperate to meet quarterly targets. Not evil — incompetent and poorly supervised. Offered Terry Hobbs a standard soul deal outside a Subway restaurant at 11:47pm. Terry said no. Azrameth signed the contract unilaterally and committed soul fraud.
**The victim:** Terry Hobbs, 42, accountant. Mild, bewildered, keeping a spreadsheet of his impossible luck. Knows something is wrong. Doesn't know what.
**The escalation:** Every win for Terry is a loss for someone nearby. Scratch cards → free parking → avoided accidents → buildings collapsing → the debt demands lives. Zero-sum probability. The comedy becomes horror when the operatives do the maths.

**Combat mechanics — DISCUSSED:**
- *Azrameth is pathetic in a fight:* Harm 2 at best (throws filing cabinets, flails). Not a combat threat. The real danger is the luck mechanism — while the contract exists, Terry's proximity is lethal to bystanders.
- *Document-based weakness:* The contract is the anchor. Three resolution paths, all delightful:
  - **Fire** — burn the physical contract. Severs the connection immediately. Azrameth panics. The demonic hierarchy notices. Consequences are real but delayed (collections department, which is much worse than acquisitions).
  - **Shredding** — physically destroying the document. Same effect as fire but less dramatic. Azrameth weeps.
  - **Filing it incorrectly / introducing typos** — the bureaucratic nuclear option. Deliberately corrupting the contract's terms so it no longer describes Terry Hobbs (misspell his name, change the date, alter the clause numbers). The demonic system processes the corrupted filing and rejects it as invalid. Azrameth is cited for the original fraud AND the corrupted filing. He is recalled to the Office of Acquisitions for disciplinary review. This is the *funniest* resolution and the *safest* — no hierarchy reprisal because it looks like Azrameth's own incompetence.
- *Bureaucratic damage bypasses armour:* When the operatives use the correct demonic procedure against Azrameth — formal objections, filing references, citing clause numbers — the damage ignores his armour entirely. He is contractually obligated to comply with valid procedural actions. A demon can resist a sword. A demon cannot resist a correctly filed Form 12-B. This is the mechanical joke: the scariest weapon in this mystery is a pen and the right paperwork.
- *The Appeals Office:* A liminal space that looks like a DMV. There is a queue. The clerk has done this ten thousand times. Processing takes "three to six business centuries" unless expedited. Expediting requires Azrameth's personal seal (on his clipboard). Getting the clipboard is the physical challenge of the mystery.

**Custom move (draft):**
> **File a Formal Objection:** When you present a correctly completed demonic procedural form to Azrameth or the Appeals Office, roll +Sharp. On a 10+: the filing is accepted. Azrameth takes 3-harm (ignore-armour, bureaucratic) and is compelled to comply with the ruling. On a 7–9: the filing has a procedural error — the clerk identifies it and gives you one chance to correct it, but Azrameth has time to act before the correction is processed. On a miss: the filing is rejected. The appeals process notes that PORTAL has "engaged with the contractual framework" — which, technically, makes you a party to the dispute. Azrameth grins for the first time.

**John Johnson note:** John is the natural character to discover that PORTAL's database contains two previous cases filed as "contractual anomaly" and marked "DO NOT PURSUE" by Leech. He would be right that this is significant. He would be wrong about why.

### The Fey Court's Interest in Alan
**Status:** UNREVIEWED
**What it is:** The fey watcher in S03 came to assess whether Alan matches a description. Someone in the fey world has a prophecy about what Alan will become — or a record of what he is. The question is: which court, and what do they want?
**Two possible directions:** (A) Alan was taken for a reason — not randomly swapped, but specifically chosen. Something about his human family, or his location, or his potential. The fey child left in his place was also specifically chosen. This implies a deliberate exchange rather than an opportunistic one. (B) Alan is not the one they're watching. They're watching what grows near him — the thin places he accumulates, the threshold maps, the resonance. Alan is a sensor they planted in the human world and have been monitoring ever since.
**When to surface:** Episode 8 is the planned moment. But the watcher's motivation should be decided before then so the keeper can play it consistently.

### MESA's Funders — Why They Want Foresight
**Status:** UNREVIEWED
**What's canon:** MESA has financial backers paying for access to prophetic intelligence — operational knowledge of future events sourced from temporally displaced consciousnesses. This is confirmed in the arc roadmap and will be evidenced in the Episode 5 heist documents. The funders exist. The product is foresight.
**What's open:** Why do they want it? Three possible angles:
- **(A) Institutional** — a government body or intelligence service wanting advance knowledge of events. Clean, procedural, the most boring version.
- **(B) Financial** — investors positioning against markets, companies, or events. More human, harder to stop.
- **(C) Personal** — individuals who received a prophecy about themselves and are using MESA to either accelerate it or prevent it. The most interesting version: people deliberately applying the binding mechanic to lock themselves into desired futures. The mirror image of Cameron, who withholds prophecy to keep people free.
**The irony worth keeping regardless of which angle:** MESA's funders — whoever they are — are doing the opposite of what Cameron does. He withholds knowledge to preserve freedom. They seek knowledge to constrain outcomes. Two responses to the same mechanic.

## EPISODE IDEAS — DEVELOPED

### The Shōjō — Emergency Override / Episode 4 Candidate
**Status:** DISCUSSED
**Type:** Traditional supernatural creature — Japanese folklore. Emergency override case (not queue-chosen).
**Trigger:** Killings near the PORTAL facility. CAMPBELL flags it as imminent — proximity, civilian risk, can't wait. The hunters don't choose this. It overrides whatever they were planning.
**MESA angle:** The timing may not be coincidental. MESA has been modelling PORTAL's deployment patterns. The Shōjō's emergence — or its proximity to the lab — may have been engineered or exploited by MESA to pull the field team off the open cases. While the hunters fight the creature, MESA moves on Gordon Avery and Nadia's window. Whether MESA caused this or merely used it should stay ambiguous.

**The creature:** A sea spirit associated with alcohol, excess, and melancholy. Ten feet tall, red-haired, perpetually, magnificently drunk. Drawn to extremes of genuine emotion — sorrow OR joy. It drinks what people feel. When it drinks too much, the person empties.

**Why it's near Baltimore:** A Japanese shipping company has been running cultural exchange events at the Baltimore Inner Harbor — sake tastings, a floating restaurant on a converted barge. The Shōjō attached to a cask of ceremonial sake because the sorrow in it was real. The brewer's daughter had died. The grief was in the liquid.

**The deaths:** Three so far, nothing in common except emotional intensity. A man celebrating a promotion — died laughing, cardiac arrest with an expression of absolute happiness. A woman grieving her mother — found peaceful, no cause, just gone. A dockworker who was both furious and relieved simultaneously. The inconsistency is the clue.

**The comedy:** It is staggering through the Docklands at 2am knocking over bins and singing. CAMPBELL's briefing includes three 911 calls describing "a very large ginger man" who "seemed to be having a wonderful time." The police are looking for a pub crawler. PORTAL knows better.

**Hunter hooks:**
- *Rex:* builds something to track its emotional feeding signature. First proper gadget-focused combat.
- *Reed:* keeps the dockworkers calm, manages the civilian situation. His competence in a crisis is the asset.
- *Alan:* glamour won't work — the Shōjō sees through pretense because it feeds on what's real. He runs interference with police and the shipping company instead. Useful but humbling.
- *Sven:* the wild card. What does the Shōjō perceive when it tries to drink from someone who is dead? Does it taste grief? Nothing? Does it recoil? This is a Sven reflection moment — his nature matters in a way it hasn't before.

**Resolution:** It can be fought directly — this is the palate cleanser, there should be a real fight with a weakness to discover. But it can also be reasoned with if someone approaches it with genuine emotional honesty. Both paths are valid. The fight path requires discovering its weakness. The diplomacy path requires someone being truly, uncomfortably honest about what they feel. Neither is easy.

**Combat mechanics — DISCUSSED:**
- *Hard to hurt:* The Shōjō has armour 2 and sees through pretence. Hunters need moves or gear dealing >2 harm to actually damage it. Rex's gadgets are the natural answer — first proper gadget-focused combat. Standard sidearms and blunt force barely register.
- *Knowing the weakness reduces armour:* Once the hunters identify the ceremonial sake as the Shōjō's vulnerability (from Yuki Tanaka or the cask itself), its armour drops by 1. The creature becomes readable — its movements predictable because the hunters understand what it is. Stack with the sake-circle ritual for full effect.
- *Drunk = better perception:* The Shōjō exists in an emotional frequency humans can't normally perceive clearly. Alcohol lowers the filter. Hunters who are genuinely drunk gain +1 to Investigate and Act Under Pressure rolls involving the Shōjō — they can *see* it properly, track its feeding patterns, predict where it moves. The downside: -1 to Tough rolls (coordination) and any miss while drunk deals 1 extra harm to the hunter. Being drunk in a monster fight is an advantage and a liability simultaneously. This is the kind of mechanical weirdness MoTW thrives on.
- *Sven interaction:* What does the Shōjō taste when it tries to drink from someone who is already dead? If it recoils, that's information. If it doesn't recoil — if it tastes something — that's a different kind of information. Either way, Sven is immune to the emotional drain but the creature's reaction tells him something about his own nature.

**Custom move (draft):**
> **Drink With the Spirit:** When you share sake with the Shōjō (or are genuinely, properly drunk in its presence), roll +Weird. On a 10+: you perceive the creature fully — its emotional frequency, its feeding pattern, its sadness. Take +1 forward against it and ask the Keeper one question about its nature. On a 7–9: you see it, but it sees you too. It knows what you feel. The Keeper tells you what it perceives in you. On a miss: it drinks from you. Take 2-harm (ignore-armour) as it consumes whatever you were feeling most intensely.

### The Locked Ward — Reed/Rex Arc Case Candidate
**Status:** UNREVIEWED
**Type:** Morally grey entity case. Reed/Rex arc anchor.
**Premise:** A psychiatric ward patient hasn't spoken in nine months. He draws constantly — architectural floor plans, elevations, structural details. They are accurate: he has drawn the interior of a decommissioned PORTAL-adjacent research facility he has never visited. Leech knows the building.

**The entity:** Not in the patient. In the building. The decommissioned facility was mothballed with a consciousness-preservation experiment still running. It has been broadcasting weakly for three years. The patient is a receiver. Others in the ward are starting to draw too.

**The Reed/Rex pressure:** Leech's directive to Reed was specifically about this facility. He knew it might surface. He told Reed to contain it — not investigate it, contain it. Rex wants to investigate. The directive says no.

**Comedy:** The ward staff think it's savant syndrome. One nurse has framed several drawings. The head psychiatrist has written a paper on it and is presenting at a conference next month. He is about to become famous for a paper that is entirely, catastrophically wrong.

**2005 detail:** The facility's records are on physical media — floppy disks and early CD-ROMs in a storage unit unopened since 2002. Rex has to find a computer that can read them.

### The Wake — Sven Arc Case Candidate
**Status:** UNREVIEWED
**Type:** Altered-state thin-boundary case. Sven arc anchor.
**Premise:** An Irish wake in a Baltimore pub. The deceased was a fiddle player, 83, died of natural causes. By the second night, the dead man is playing. Not his ghost. Something that knew him — from the thin places where music goes when nobody is listening.

**The altered-state context:** Three days of grief, whiskey, music, and no sleep. The boundary is being thinned by entirely human practice — the oldest way. No artefact. No MESA. Just a room full of people who loved someone and won't stop singing.

**The entity:** Plays the dead man's tunes perfectly. Also plays tunes that don't exist yet — melodies nobody has heard, which the other musicians pick up without realising they're learning something impossible. Rex's scanner reads the melodies as structured BIM data.

**Sven's moment:** The entity recognises him — not with the Cartographer's confusion or Edmund's warmth, but with the specific recognition of someone who has been in the same place Sven is now. It communicates through melody. The tunes it plays when Sven is in the room are different. They are an answer to a question he hasn't asked yet.

**Prophecy layer:** One impossible tune contains a BIM-frequency signature that, when decoded, describes a sequence of events that hasn't happened yet. The tune is a prophecy. The musicians who learned it carry it in their muscle memory. Playing it again would activate the binding mechanic. Sven decides: does he tell them to stop, or let the music exist?

**Comedy:** The pub landlord is trying to run a normal business. The wake has been going for 52 hours. He is out of Guinness. There are fourteen fiddle players in his back room and one of them is, he's fairly sure, dead. He would like to close at a reasonable hour.

**2005 detail:** No mobile reception — 2005 coverage gaps in the basement pub. The team is genuinely isolated from the lab.
