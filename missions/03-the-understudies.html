The Understudies

A Monster of the Week Mystery
Scene Preparation for the Keeper
// WORKING TITLE — UPDATE AFTER SESSION
It started with Shakespeare. It always starts with something that was true before it became dangerous. Proteus betrays his closest friend by becoming someone he isn't. Silvia is the thing men transform themselves to deserve. Clara and Edmund played those roles for three seasons until the roles started playing them. They didn't choose to stay. But they didn't choose to leave either. And in the end that distinction mattered less than they expected.

Premise & Scene Structure

Three understudies at the Meridian Theatre Company have lost themselves entirely — responding only to character names, showing no organic cause, persistent for eleven days. The remaining understudies are still performing. Saturday is tomorrow. The same mechanism that took the first three is active in every rehearsal, and it has been building for nearly two years.

MESA funded the theatre. The same shell company chain as Aldermoor. Someone is cultivating thin-boundary environments, and the hunters have just been handed the third one in a row.

The case has branching investigation paths. Each route gives different pieces. There is a real mystery here and it requires actual investigation — the players cannot rush directly to the climax and understand what they're resolving.

Scene sequence: Hook (Lab Briefing) → Cold Open → Scene 1 (The Hospital) → Scene 2 (The Theatre) → Scene 3 (Behind the Stage) → Climax (Saturday's Performance)

The Play — The Two Gentlemen of Verona

The Meridian Theatre Company is small — twelve actors, serious classical repertory. The Two Gentlemen of Verona has been their anchor production for three seasons: the story of Valentine and Proteus, two friends whose loyalty fractures when one transforms himself — through jealousy, cowardice, and desire — into someone his closest friend no longer recognises. Silvia is the woman both men believe they love. The play is entirely about identity, disguise, and the cost of becoming someone you're not.

The mechanism does not affect all twelve equally. Two conditions must coincide: sufficient accumulated performance time in a role originated by Clara or Edmund, and regular physical contact with the prompt book during rehearsal. Actors in supporting roles who handle the book rarely are not at risk in the same way. Actors who joined this season have not built enough repetition. The three hospitalised understudies represent the worst intersection: highest book-contact, most seasons, covering the two lead roles. Four active understudies are at lesser but measurable risk. Two company members show no signs at all.

Clara Voss played Silvia. Edmund Farrow played Proteus. They had done so for three consecutive seasons — the same roles, the same lines, the same choices, night after night, until the roles were as habitual as breathing. That is what the mechanism required. Not talent. Not intention. Just repetition until the boundary between performer and role stopped being enforced.

THE CHAIN OF INHABITATION — Keeper Reference

The mechanism runs in layers, oldest first:

  1. Proteus (fictional) inhabits Edmund — over three seasons of performance, the character bleeds in. Edmund starts knowing his lines before he reads them. Making choices in rehearsal that feel like memory, not invention. He thinks it's excellence. It is and it isn't.
  2. Silvia (fictional) inhabits Clara — same process, same timeline. Clara notices first. She writes it down. Her notebooks shift from rehearsal notes to something stranger.
  3. Clara dies. Edmund dies. — Their consciousness stays in the theatre. The fictional characters they were inhabited by disperse without a host. Clara and Edmund remain, in the building, in the roles, unable or unwilling to leave.
  4. Clara and Edmund inhabit the understudies — when the new performers step into Silvia and Proteus, Clara and Edmund find the path of least resistance. Not a choice, exactly. More like water finding a channel.
  5. But the fictional echoes also remain — Proteus and Silvia are still faintly present in the building. The third hospitalised understudy (playing a minor role with earlier scene overlap) was caught by the fictional residue rather than by Clara or Edmund directly. This understudy responds only to their character name, repeats lines from Act II, and cannot contextualise their situation. They are inhabited by a fictional consciousness — coherent within its own logic, confused by anything outside it. This is the clue that tells the hunters there is a second layer: Clara and Edmund know they are dead; this third understudy does not know they are a person.

Clara's distribution: Clara is not simultaneously present in multiple understudies at full strength. She shifts — heavier during performance, lighter between. The understudy she is most present in is Sophie Marsh, who plays Silvia in Saturday's show. A second Clara-understudy (hospitalised) shows partial displacement — present but incomplete. Rex's scanner would show fluctuating levels, not fixed ones. Clara is choosing where to be most present. She has chosen Saturday.

The understudies at risk are specifically those playing Silvia and Proteus — the parts Clara and Edmund inhabited. The rest of the company has minor effects at most: knowing lines they haven't learned, déjà vu in rehearsal. Unsettling, but not clinical. The roles were the charge, not the building.

Clara Voss and Edmund Farrow — Full Backstory

Clara Voss, 41. Edmund Farrow, 38. Eight years working together at the Meridian. They met when Edmund joined the company as a young lead and Clara was already its anchor. Started as colleagues, became close friends, were — for about two years in the middle — something more. It ended by mutual agreement when the work started to suffer from it. They remained each other's most trusted creative partner. Edmund called Clara when a scene wasn't working. Clara called Edmund when she was frightened. Nobody at the theatre was surprised by their closeness. Nobody knew its full extent.

When Clara died, Edmund identified the body. When Edmund died four months later, there was nobody left who had known both of them that well.

Clara Voss — Death & Backstory Deceased — 14 months ago

What she wanted: to see the touring production she'd been scheduled to direct. She had been planning it for two years. She never got to.

By month 7 of the mechanism building, Clara's notebooks had stopped being rehearsal notes. She was recording something she didn't have a name for: knowing lines she hadn't been given, remembering blocking for scenes she hadn't rehearsed, feeling — in specific, documented moments — like Silvia was making decisions that Clara then enacted rather than the reverse. She thought she was losing her mind. She told Edmund some of it. Not all.

The death: Late night at the theatre after a Saturday double bill. She fell from the upper rehearsal room staircase — a staircase she had climbed hundreds of times. Ruled accidental. She was alone; no witnesses. The most plausible reconstruction: she missed a step in the dark. The actual explanation: for approximately two seconds, she was Silvia, and Silvia has never climbed those stairs. Edmund was the person who identified her the following morning. He told the company himself.

After death: Clara did not disperse. She was too embedded in the building, in the role, in the work she hadn't finished. She remained — aware, present, watching. She watched Edmund perform for four more months. She watched him deteriorate. She could not reach him. She has been in the theatre ever since, and she is more aware of her situation than Edmund is. She has had longer to think about it. When Sven enters her dressing room, she has a question she has been carrying for fourteen months: Is this what it's always like — being somewhere without being anywhere? Or does it get easier to hold? She has found something like stillness. She doesn't know if it is peace or just the absence of alternatives. She is not asking for reassurance. She is asking for honest information from someone who has been doing this longer than she has.

Edmund Farrow — Death & Backstory Deceased — 8 months ago

What he wanted: to finish the run. To not leave things unfinished the way he left them when he failed to tell Clara, in time, that he was frightened too.

After Clara died, Edmund insisted on performing. The company tried to give him time off. He refused. In retrospect — and Petra Novak has thought about this — he was never fully present offstage after her death, and more present than he'd ever been onstage. At the time, everyone called it grief channelled into the work. It was that, and it was also Proteus taking over in the gaps where Edmund had retreated.

The death: Motorway, clear afternoon conditions, six months after Clara. The car drifted into the central reservation. No skid marks — he didn't brake. Witnesses said it looked like a microsleep. It was not a microsleep. For the length of a breath, Proteus was driving, and Proteus has never seen a motorway. Edmund was back in the next instant, but the car was already moving and the barrier was already there.

After death: Edmund arrived in the theatre within days. Clara was already there. They have been in the building together for eight months, able to sense each other but unable to communicate except through the understudies. Edmund is less aware of his situation than Clara — he is happy to be back, delighted by the performances, only mildly troubled by the understudies' distress. Clara finds this maddening. This tension between them is available for the hunters to surface.

The Artefact — The Prompt Book

Eighteen months ago, the Meridian Theatre Company acquired a 19th-century prompt book from a private estate auction as a prestige piece for their anniversary season. It is displayed in a glass case in the foyer and was used periodically in rehearsal — handled by Clara and Edmund, referenced in blocking notes, occasionally placed on the prop table during run-throughs.

The book belonged to a celebrated performer of the Victorian era who died mid-performance — on stage, in front of a full house, playing a role he had performed hundreds of times. The death was cardiac. The performance was, by all contemporary accounts, the finest he had ever given. He was, in the words of every witness, more completely inhabited by his role that night than anyone had ever seen him.

The prompt book is not haunted. The actor is not in it. What it holds is older and less personal: the accumulated residue of an audience's belief across decades of performance — the emotional charge of thousands of people accepting, night after night, that one man was someone else. When that man died fully inside his role, the transfer left a mark on the material object it had passed through. The book is a BIM-saturated artefact. It holds liminal charge the way certain stones hold heat.

For 150 years it was inert. It needed two things to activate: an active performance context, and sufficient ambient BIM charge. The Cameron Dell consciousness transfer provided the latter. The ripple from that event — the first successful large-scale consciousness transfer — propagated outward through thin-boundary locations across the city. The prompt book was one of the first sites Agatha Dell's researchers detected it, through routine BIM-signature scanning of auction catalogues and estate records. MESA placed the Solstice Property Group grant before the theatre had any idea what they had.

KEEPER NOTE — What the Artefact Is Not

The prompt book is not an entity. It is not a ghost. It is a conduit — a material object that amplifies the consciousness-transfer mechanism already latent in performance. The mechanism is the performance itself: audience belief as BIM amplifier, the act of thousands of people fully accepting that one person is another, until the boundary in the performers' minds becomes genuinely permeable. The book makes this process faster and more complete. Without it the mechanism would eventually develop in this space — but it would take decades of repetition rather than months.

The book is also the resolution key. When the hunters find it and understand what it is, they have a choice: remove it from the performance space before Saturday's show (which weakens the mechanism significantly but does not resolve it — Clara and Edmund are already present), or use it actively in the theatrical farewell (place it on the stage, acknowledge what it is, let the mechanism complete with consent rather than despite it). The second option is the cleaner resolution. Rex will understand why within a scene of examining it.

COLD OPEN — Someone Had to Keep Things Going

Tone: Unsettling in a specific way — not violent, not ghostly. Just entirely, calmly wrong. A person who is fully present, fully articulate, and completely not themselves. Edmund's warmth makes it worse, not better.

The ward is quiet. A woman — mid-thirties, still in her coat, handbag over one arm — sits in the chair beside the bed. She has been here every day for eleven days. She is Daniel Ashworth's sister, and she has brought him a book he likes and some fruit he won't touch, and she is running out of things to say.

The man in the bed is sitting upright, hands folded, watching the window. He turns when she speaks his name.

"Danny." She says it gently. The way you say a name when you've stopped expecting it to work. "It's me. It's Sarah."

He looks at her with genuine warmth — really looks, the way actors learn to look, taking in an audience. Then he smiles.

"You brought the wrong book," he says. His voice is different. Not wrong — just older, settled, lived-in in a way that doesn't match his face. "Daniel doesn't like that one. He only keeps it because your mother gave it to him. He'd never tell you that."

She goes very still.

"He told me quite a lot, in the first few days," the man in the bed continues, pleasantly. "We've had a lot of time to talk. I'm Edmund, by the way. I know that doesn't help."

"Edmund Farrow died eight months ago," she says.

"Yes." He folds his hands again. "Daniel's fine. He's just — resting. I've been keeping things going. Someone had to."

Backstage smells like greasepaint and timber and something older. The lights dim in the house. The audience quiets. And in the space between the last murmur and the first cue, there is a quality of attention in the building that has nothing to do with the four hundred people in the seats.

Clara and Edmund are ready. They have been waiting for this.

What the Mechanism Requires

The role-anchored consciousness transfer is sustained by performance — specifically, by an audience believing in the performance. The actors stayed because they couldn't leave. They can leave, but only through an act that is structurally parallel to how they arrived: a farewell performed within the logic of theatre, witnessed by an audience, that both Clara and Edmund accept as a conclusion.

This is not an exorcism. It is a closing night. The understudies need to perform a scene that works as a goodbye — and the deceased actors need to understand that it is goodbye, not abandonment. The mechanism releases when they choose to let go, not when they are forced out.

Resolution Paths

BEST ENDING — The Theatrical Farewell Sven goes onstage. Not as an actor — as himself. He is the only one who can make the argument that letting go is not the same as ending, because he has not let go and he knows what that costs. In the logic of performance, his presence onstage constitutes a scene — and the audience, not understanding what they're watching, believes it completely. Clara and Edmund hear what he says. They choose the ending. The understudies return to themselves, disoriented but whole, in front of a confused and deeply moved audience. Petra Novak improvises a curtain call. She is extraordinary under pressure.
PARTIAL ENDING — The Interrupted Performance The hunters stop the performance before it completes — pulling understudies offstage, disrupting the mechanism. Clara and Edmund are forced out rather than choosing to leave. The understudies recover, but not completely: they retain traces of the other actors' memories and instincts. Not damaging, not dangerous — but permanent. Sophie Marsh will be a better actor for it. She is not sure how to feel about that.
DIFFICULT ENDING — MESA Gets the Documentation The hunters resolve the mechanism but allow MESA to observe. Rook watches from the stalls and documents everything. The understudies recover. The resolution methodology is now in MESA's possession. The next thin-boundary environment they fund will have a cleaner exit strategy. They have learned from PORTAL's solution.
BAD ENDING — The Performance Completes If the hunters cannot intervene before curtain, Saturday's show runs to completion. Four understudies lose themselves during the performance. The audience goes home believing they've seen something remarkable. They have. By the time the lights come up, Sophie Marsh and three others respond only to their character names. The clock fires.

Tone Reminders

  • Clara and Edmund are not villains. They are people who loved their work and couldn't let go. Play them with warmth and specificity — they are good at what they do, they are glad to be back, and they do not fully understand the cost.
