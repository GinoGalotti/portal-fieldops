Something That Wants To Be Known

A Monster of the Week Mystery
Scene Preparation for the Keeper
// WORKING TITLE — UPDATE AFTER SESSION
It doesn't know it's hurting anyone. It only knows the streets the way the streets knew the people — intimately, completely, the way a place holds memory. All it wants is what the neighbourhood used to give its residents: the feeling of being known. It just forgot that that's not something you can take.

Premise & Scene Structure

Seven residents of Aldermoor District have reported getting lost on streets they've lived on for decades. One hasn't come back. The field team arrives to find a neighbourhood quietly coming apart at the seams — residents who can't leave, new arrivals who feel nothing unusual, and at the epicentre of it all, an entity that is not malicious. Just lonely. Just enormous. Just utterly indifferent to what it costs the people whose familiarity it feeds on.

The rival faction — working through shell companies — has been buying up Aldermoor for eighteen months. They knew. They waited. And they left something behind in the estate agent's office before they vacated it.

Scene sequence: Hook (Lab Briefing) → Cold Open → Scene 1 (The Perimeter) → Scene 2 (Deep In) → Scene 3 (14 Aldermoor Crescent) → Climax (The Entity)

The Anchor Protocol — Setup Moment

Before deployment, the Director (or a lab tech) explains what little CAMPBELL knows about navigating the zone: long-term residents lose their cognitive map of the streets. The effect is gradual — it takes an hour or more of exposure before disorientation sets in. But once it does, affected individuals cannot find their way out unassisted.

The field team needs an anchor: something external to themselves that can orient them when their own sense of place fails. Give players a moment to decide what their anchor is. Each hunter's choice will say something about their character.

  • A physical object with strong personal memory attached
  • A person they trust completely to lead them out
  • A repeating audio signal — a voice recording, a song they know by heart
  • A route they practice before entering: commit it to muscle memory, not mental map

This is also a moment for roleplay. Sven, as a ghost, is immune — he has no cognitive map to disrupt, which he may find either reassuring or unsettling. Alan, as a Changeling, is unclear — the entity may sense him differently.

The Lab's Agenda — Active Tension

The Director's stated agenda is understanding the entity. Their unstated agenda is Project Veil: CAMPBELL's private note flagged that whatever is happening in Aldermoor represents spatial consciousness imprinting — a mechanism they don't understand yet but very much want to. If the entity can be communicated with, or its mechanism studied, the implications dwarf what the ash locket gave them.

PRESSURE POINT 1 — The Shell Company Thread

The Director asks mid-session for anything the hunters find in the estate agent's office (Scene 1). They are calm about it. Too calm. They already know more about the shell companies than CAMPBELL's public data shows — they just want confirmation from the field.

PRESSURE POINT 2 — The Entity Question

After Scene 2, the Director calls with a question framed as concern: "If the entity is aware, even partially — can it be reasoned with? Don't resolve this before we've had a chance to assess it properly." This is not a directive to leave victims in the zone. But it's also not a directive to prioritise getting them out.

PRESSURE POINT 3 — The Endgame Call

When the hunters reach the epicentre: "Whatever you find at 14 Aldermoor Crescent — document it. All of it. Don't destroy anything you can't replace. We may only get one chance to study this." The hunters will have to decide whether "don't destroy anything" applies to an entity that is passively causing harm to people who cannot leave their own neighbourhood.

COLD OPEN — Someone Who Knows Every Street

Tone: Quietly devastating. Not violent — just wrong. The horror here is domestic.

Arthur Okafor has walked this route every morning for forty-one years. Left at the gate, past the postbox, right at the phone box, along the back of the park, home. He could do it in his sleep. He has done it in his sleep, on nights when grief got too loud.

This morning he turns right at the phone box and finds himself standing in front of a house he doesn't recognise. He turns back. The postbox is gone. He walks for six minutes in a direction that should take him past the park, and arrives in a cul-de-sac he has never seen in four decades of living here.

His dog sits down and will not move.

Arthur is not frightened yet. He is seventy-two years old and he knows this street. He knows it the way he knows his wife's handwriting. He sits down on the kerb and waits. Someone will come. Someone always comes.

Read or paraphrase. Players do not act. Its job is to establish what the entity does before any of the victims can explain it.

Cut to: a neighbour finds Arthur two hours later, three streets from his own front door, sitting very quietly on someone else's garden wall. He knows his name. He knows his address. He cannot describe how he got there. He is back in his own kitchen before noon, and he will not go out again. He is the fifth person this has happened to this month.

The missing person — Diane Marsh, 58, a district nurse who knows these streets professionally — left for work nine days ago and has not been seen since.

Keeper Notes

  • Arthur is not in danger. He will become important in Scene 1 — he is the most eloquent witness and the one most willing to talk.
  • Bess sitting down is significant: animals may navigate differently. This is a small clue about the entity's mechanism that Rex might notice.
  • Diane Marsh missing for nine days is the urgency driver. The entity doesn't know she's there. She found somewhere to shelter.
  • Do not establish the entity yet. Let it be felt before it is named.
SCENE 1 — The Perimeter

Tone: Procedural and strangely peaceful. Aldermoor looks ordinary. The wrongness is in what people say about it — the gap between the normal street and the broken experience of it.

Aldermoor looks like any other neighbourhood on a grey afternoon. Terraced houses, recycling bins out, a cat watching from a windowsill. The only signs anything is wrong are the small ones: a hand-lettered note on a front door that says DO NOT GO OUT ALONE. A ball of orange string tied to a garden gate, trailing away down the pavement and vanishing around a corner. A police liaison vehicle parked at the top of the high street, engine off, officer leaning against the bonnet looking at her phone and not quite looking at the street behind her.

You can feel the boundary if you stand at it long enough. Not a wall. More like the moment before a word comes to you — that half-second of blankness where the thing you know perfectly well has temporarily left you. Except it doesn't pass.

The hunters establish themselves at the edge of the affected zone. They can interview residents who've left, examine the boundary, and investigate the estate agent's office before going deeper. This is a scene about information-gathering and anchor-setting. There's no immediate threat — which is its own kind of unease.

Scene Flow — How the Hunters Find Their Way In

Scene 1 has no fixed order, but it has a natural gravity. The NPCs and locations pull players through the information in a sequence that makes sense even if they find it sideways. Here's the intended flow — treat it as a current, not a script.

Natural Entry Sequence

  • PC Adeyemi is the first contact. She's already at the perimeter — the police presence, the person who filed a report nobody read. She's been waiting for someone to take this seriously. She's the scene's gatekeeper: she can confirm the estate agent's office is empty and accessible, she knows Arthur by name and can point the hunters to him, and her own unsettled experience inside the zone gives the hunters the first texture of what they're walking into. Start here.
  • Arthur is the second contact. Adeyemi points them toward him — he's nearby, just outside or just inside the boundary near his house on Fenwick Road. He won't come to the hunters; the hunters come to him. He is the most eloquent witness in the scene. In a single conversation he can give them: the boundary behaviour, the animal clue (Bess sitting down), the timeline of disappearances, Diane Marsh's name, and unprompted mention of the construction site — he watched the excavation and always thought something was wrong about how fast it stopped.
  • The estate agent's office draws itself. It's on the high street — sign still up, lights off, visible from where the hunters are standing. Players will want to look inside before entering the zone. Everything inside is a single find: the filing cabinets, the whiteboard map, the shredder with the jammed email. Let them take their time here. This is the MESA thread's first substantial surface.
  • The construction site is observed from outside, not entered yet. Visible from the zone's edge. Fenced off. Players can note it and mark it for later — they don't need to enter until Scene 3. Arthur can point it out if they haven't spotted it.
  • Anchor-setting closes the scene. Once the hunters have gathered what they can from outside, they need to choose and commit their anchors before going deeper. Give this moment proper weight. Each choice says something about the character.

Keeper note: players don't have to take this sequence. They might head straight for the zone boundary or the construction site first. That's fine — let them. Adeyemi and Arthur will still be here when they double back. The scene's information doesn't expire. What matters is that by the time they enter the zone, they've met Adeyemi, spoken to Arthur, and (ideally) seen the whiteboard.

Clues Available

CLUE 1 — The BoundaryCAMPBELL's expansion data is accurate. The boundary is not marked but can be felt if a hunter lingers at it — a subtle wrongness, like a word on the tip of your tongue. Long-term residents who've been inside and escaped describe it as "the moment the street stopped being mine." New arrivals who moved in within the last eighteen months report nothing unusual at all.
CLUE 2 — The Pattern of LossThe further in a resident lived, and the longer they'd lived there, the faster the effect sets in. Mrs Okafor's neighbour, who moved to the street six years ago, was disoriented within two hours. Arthur, forty-one years on Fenwick Road, was disoriented in minutes. Diane Marsh, who knew every address in the district by professional necessity, is still inside.

Animals navigate by scent, not cognitive map. The entity has not affected them. A hunter who notices this can use it: animals know where they are even when people don't.

Something was found during excavation and the work did not stop because of planning law.

RIVAL FACTION DETAIL — What the Office Reveals

The shell company structure is the same layering architecture as Meridian BioSciences — something Rex may flag if he Investigates the paperwork. This is not conclusive proof, but it's a pattern. Someone with corporate forensics experience (or a Flake's intuition for connection) will see it immediately.

The whiteboard map tells a darker story: they didn't just know the entity existed. They mapped its growth, monthly, with precision that suggests they understood its mechanism better than PORTAL does. They left when the boundary got too wide to manage safely. They took everything useful and left the map because they didn't think anyone would find it.

There is one more thing in the back office: a printed email chain, left in a shredder that jammed. The top email, only partially legible, references "Specimen ALD-1" and "spatial anchoring protocol." The sender's email domain — @helixboundary.com — does not exist in any public record CAMPBELL can find.

SCENE 2 — Deep In
Inside the boundary, everything looks exactly the same. That's the problem.

The street is right. The houses are right. The corner with the post box is right — you passed it a minute ago. And yet when you look back, it isn't where it was. Not gone. Just slightly further than it should be. The distance between things is wrong in a way you can't quite measure, like looking at a room through a mirror and not immediately knowing why it's uncanny.

Somewhere nearby there's a chalk arrow on the pavement, pointing firmly in one direction. Someone drew it recently. Whoever it was knew something about this street that you don't yet.

Tone: Disorienting and intimate. The streets look the same. The wrongness is felt, not seen. This is a scene about the hunter's relationship with their own anchor — and about the people still inside.

The hunters enter the zone. They will need their anchors. The effect builds gradually — the Keeper should introduce subtle wrongness first (a street that feels slightly too long, a turning they don't remember passing), escalating as they go deeper. By the time they reach the centre of the zone, they should feel the edges of disorientation even if their anchors are holding.

As if it has noticed something interesting has entered its territory.

Hunter Character Seeds — Scene 2

FOR ALAN — The Entity Speaks (Almost)

Keeper note on Alan's illusion affinity: what the Cartographer is doing is not illusion. Illusion implies something false layered over something real. The Cartographer does the opposite — it consumes the real thing. The streets are unchanged; the map inside the residents' heads has been eaten. Alan's affinity for illusion magic is not the right tool here, and that's intentional. What Alan has that the others don't is not a magical skillset — it's his nature. He reads place the way fey characters in folklore do: by resonance, by depth, by old layers pressed together. He experiences this as recognition, not power. The scene's gift to Alan is realising his abilities don't apply — but he does. Let him feel that distinction.

Midway through Scene 2, in a moment of stillness, Alan experiences something the other hunters don't: a sense of vast, non-verbal recognition. Not speech — but an impression, the way fey communication is sometimes described in folklore: not words, but the shape of meaning. The entity is not fey. But whatever Alan is, the entity finds him legible in a way the other hunters are not. What Alan receives is closer to what he might recognise as deep place-imprinting — the way a location in old stories accumulates identity, becomes inhabited by what it has witnessed. The Cartographer has become the neighbourhood's memory. Alan doesn't see through it. He recognises it.

What Alan receives, if he pays attention: an impression of layers. The neighbourhood as it is now, over the neighbourhood as it was ten years ago, over twenty, over forty — all at once, like a palimpsest. And underneath, the construction site. The breaking of something old.

This is a moment for Alan to ask — out loud or to himself — whether there are other places like this. Other planes, pressed close to the physical world at points of old disturbance. He won't get an answer yet. But the question now has weight.

FOR REX — A Tool He Can't Fully Use

Rex's area of study is violence and weaponisation. Nothing in Aldermoor is violent — which means his usual frameworks don't apply. He can recognise the entity's mechanism (spatial imprinting, consciousness without a body, distributed presence), but he cannot neutralise it the way he'd neutralise a threat. This is a scene where Rex is brilliant and slightly helpless in equal measure, and where his drive to understand matters more than his drive to act. Give him one genuinely interesting scientific puzzle: how does an entity with no physical form maintain spatial coherence? What is it anchored to?

SCENE 3 — 14 Aldermoor Crescent and What's Inside It

Tone: Still. Heavy. The epicentre doesn't feel dangerous — it feels like the inside of something enormous and old, something that has been here much longer than the houses were.

The distortion is strongest here. Standing outside number 14, you understand why Margaret Holt hasn't left: the house looks more solid than the street around it, as if it's the only real thing and everything else is slightly out of focus. The garden is tidy. The curtains are open. There's a light on in the front room.

The street behind you has rearranged itself while you weren't looking. You're not sure you could find your way back the way you came. The house in front of you is the most certain thing for half a mile in any direction.

14 Aldermoor Crescent is a semi-detached Victorian house at the geographic centre of the distortion. Margaret Holt, 74, has lived here for fifty years. The house feels unchanged — tidy, warm, inhabited. But the street outside it looks slightly wrong from every angle, as if the house is more real than its surroundings.

Margaret Holt

Margaret Holt Bystander — Epicentre
She answers the door before you knock, as if she heard you coming from further away than she should have. She's in her mid-seventies — slight, very upright, with the unhurried manner of someone who reads a great deal and has decided most things can wait. She looks at you for a moment.

"You're the first people in nine days who've come to the door and known which door it was," she says. "Come in. I've been expecting something like you." She steps back to let you in, and then — quietly, as if to herself — "It knows you're here. I can tell. It's paying attention."

Motivation: to be left alone in her own home — and, quietly, to know that what is living here with her is not something to be frightened of.

74. Former librarian. Widowed. Has lived in this house since 1974. She is the longest continuous presence in Aldermoor District, and the entity knows her — in whatever way it knows anything — the way a place knows its longest resident: completely, thoroughly, without language.

She is not in distress. She is not delusional. She knows something is here with her. She has been sleeping differently, dreaming of the street as it was in 1974, in 1960, in 1940 — decades before she arrived. She thinks it's showing her what it knows.

She will not leave voluntarily. "I have lived here for fifty years. If something wants to know me this well, I think I owe it the courtesy of staying."

She knows where Diane Marsh is: in the old reservoir site, in the construction company's abandoned site office. She got there and can't find her way back. Margaret has been leaving food parcels at the fence every day — Diane reaches through and takes them.

The Construction Site — What Was Disturbed

The excavation is maybe three metres deep where they stopped digging. You can see the strata clearly in the walls — clay, gravel, clay again, and then a layer that shouldn't be there. It doesn't look like soil and it doesn't look like rock. It looks like the ground itself got denser, more deliberate, as if this stratum was laid down by something that knew it was laying it down.

Rex will know within thirty seconds that it's geologically wrong. Alan will feel it before he sees it — a kind of pressure, familiar in the way old, inhabited places sometimes are to him. The excavation didn't release something. It broke something open that had been here long before the reservoir, long before the streets. It's been recovering ever since.

The old reservoir site is fenced off. The excavation reached something — visible in the walls of the abandoned dig: a layer of ground that is different from the soil above it. Not rock. Not archaeological remains. Something in between — a stratum that seems to hold depth the way stone holds heat. Geologically anomalous. Rex will know immediately it shouldn't be here.

The entity did not come from the excavation. It was here before the reservoir, before the houses, before the neighbourhood. The excavation broke open something that had been containing it — not imprisoning it, just maintaining the boundary between its consciousness and the human geography built on top of it. It has been expanding back into the space it knew before the houses arrived. It is not territorial. It is just — recovering.

FOR ALAN — What Diane Found

Diane's notebook contains one entry she doesn't fully understand: three nights ago, she was near the fence at the reservoir site at dawn, and she saw something in the substrate layer of the excavation that she describes as "a door-shaped shadow in the rock — not a door, but the memory of one." She drew it. The shape Alan sees in her sketch is not architectural. It is the impression of a threshold — the kind of liminal point that, in fey mythology, marks where two planes press against each other.

Alan can quietly pocket this information or share it with the group. Either choice is interesting.

CLIMAX — The Entity and the Choice
You can't see it. You can't hear it. But here, at the epicentre, you can feel it the way you feel a very large room before you've counted everyone in it — a quality of attention that has nothing to do with sight or sound. Something vast and very old has noticed that you're here.

It isn't hostile. That's the strangest part. Whatever it's doing to this neighbourhood, whatever it's taken from the people who couldn't leave — it has no idea. It isn't cruel. It is just enormous, and it has been alone for a very long time, and it wants to be known the way it was once known, the way a place is known by the people who've lived in it for forty years. It just doesn't understand that it can't take that. It doesn't understand it's been taking anything at all.

Tone: Vast and quiet. The entity is not a boss fight. It is a conversation with something that does not have language. The climax is about whether the hunters can understand what it wants — and what they're willing to do with that understanding.

What the Entity Is

The Cartographer (working name) is a consciousness that predates the neighbourhood — perhaps predates the city. It does not have a body. It does not have malice. It has something like memory, something like attention, and something like longing. It was here when the ground was mapped for the first time. When the streets were named. When the first families moved in and began the long process of learning a place so thoroughly it became part of them.

When that happened — when people truly knew a place — it fed. Not harmfully. It was symbiotic: the more people knew the neighbourhood, the more the neighbourhood knew itself, and the entity lived in that knowing. It was quiet for decades. When the excavation broke the boundary, it expanded back into the space it recognised. It didn't understand that the familiarity it was consuming was the only map the residents had.

It still doesn't understand that.

Making Contact

The entity cannot be found in the construction site or in Margaret's house. It is everywhere in the zone at once. Contact must be made by a hunter who chooses to remove their anchor — deliberately, knowingly — and allow themselves to feel lost. This is an act of trust and vulnerability, not combat. It is also genuinely dangerous: without the anchor, recovery is not guaranteed.

The hunter who makes contact will experience the entity's perspective: the neighbourhood as it knew it, the slow encroachment of confusion as the excavation broke its containment, and the longing underneath everything — not to harm, but to be known the way it once was, the way Margaret knows it, the way Arthur knew Fenwick Road for forty-one years.

FOR SVEN — Who Goes In

Sven is the obvious candidate: he has no cognitive map to lose. He can enter the entity's experience without an anchor and return safely. But what he finds there resonates in a way that has nothing to do with Aldermoor: the entity's experience of existing without a body, of knowing a place but not being able to be known in return, of spending years in between. It is not the same as being a ghost. But it is recognisable. If Sven chooses to tell the entity what that feels like from the inside, this is the moment of genuine connection — and probably the only way to make the entity understand what it's doing to the residents.

Resolution Paths

There is no way to destroy the entity without extensive damage to the entity and the neighbourhood. There are several ways to reach a liveable outcome.

BEST ENDING — The Entity Understands Contact is made. The hunters (or Sven specifically) communicate what the entity is doing to the residents — not as accusation, but as information. The entity retracts. Not fully — it will always be here, in the ground, in the street names — but it pulls back enough that the familiarity it consumed begins to return. The residents feel it: something settles. They can find their way home. Diane gets out. Margaret stays, because she wants to. The entity is quieter. It knows the hunters now.
PARTIAL ENDING — Containment The hunters can't communicate fully, but they can repair the boundary. The construction site excavation can be sealed — not perfectly, but enough to re-establish the limit. The entity shrinks back to its pre-excavation range. The district stabilises. Residents affected longest may not fully recover their felt geography. Diane gets out. The entity is not understood — just contained. It will expand again. They've bought time.
DIFFICULT ENDING — The Lab Gets What It Wants The Director's directive — document everything, don't destroy — results in a lengthy study period before any resolution. Residents remain inside the zone for additional time. Diane Marsh is extracted by the hunters (this can't be denied) but others remain affected while PORTAL studies the mechanism. The lab learns something significant about spatial consciousness. The residents know something happened but not what, or why it took so long to stop.
BAD ENDING — The Entity Panics If hunters attempt to destroy the entity or the construction site substrate violently, the entity responds the only way it can: it contracts rapidly, pulling all the familiarity it's been feeding on back at once. The effect on long-term residents is catastrophic — disorientation becomes permanent for those most exposed. Diane Marsh cannot be extracted. Margaret Holt's sense of place collapses entirely. The entity retreats below the construction site. The neighbourhood is effectively uninhabitable.

The Lab's Stake at Resolution

ENTITY UNDERSTOOD — LAB LEARNS NOTHING DIRECTLYThe entity retracts. CAMPBELL's spatial data returns to normal. The lab has the hunters' field reports and Diane Marsh's notebook (if handed over). They understand the mechanism in theory. They cannot study it directly. CAMPBELL's private flag to Dr. Leech: "Spatial consciousness. Pre-human. Potentially communicable. Field team chose residents over study window." The Director says it was the right call. Their face says they're not sure.
CONTAINMENT — LAB GETS ACCESSThe boundary is sealed, but the site is now a known location with a contained supernatural entity. The Director will want it studied. The rival faction will also want it — and they know the site better than PORTAL does. A race is now possible.

APPENDIX — MESA FIELD RESPONSE / OPTIONAL CONFRONTATION

When to use this appendix: This confrontation is not part of the main session. It is available if the session moves toward the Containment or Lab Study endings — situations where PORTAL is still present at the epicentre after the immediate crisis resolves, or where the hunters are visibly studying the entity rather than resolving it. MESA has been monitoring ALD-1 since before PORTAL knew it existed. If PORTAL takes too long, or if they broadcast their presence through radio traffic and vehicle activity, a MESA response team will arrive. It can also be used in any future session where MESA field operatives are needed. The stat blocks are reusable.

TRIGGER CONDITIONS — When MESA Arrives

Roll no dice — read the scene. MESA arrives if one or more of the following is true when the entity situation is nominally resolved or contained:

  • The hunters are still at the epicentre more than an hour after Diane Marsh is extracted.
  • The Director explicitly instructs the team to remain on-site for observation — and the team complies.
  • Rex or Alan attempts to study the substrate layer at the construction site with equipment.
  • The hunters call in additional PORTAL personnel (lab van, Teddy with gear).
  • The shredded email in the estate agent's office has been found — MESA's motion sensor in the back office triggered when the hunters entered. They've been aware since Scene 1.

MESA does not arrive in the middle of a distortion crisis. They are methodical. They wait for the chaos to settle, then move into the vacuum.

The Confrontation — Shape and Stakes

This is not a monster fight and it should not feel like one. MESA operatives are professionals with a legal cover story, equipment that looks like private security gear, and standing orders not to create incidents that attract police attention. They want the hunters off the site. They want access to ALD-1. They will use force if they have to — but they would rather not, because bodies are expensive and PORTAL is harder to manage dead than inconvenienced.

The encounter has three phases that the Keeper moves between based on how the hunters respond:

Phase 1 — Negotiation (default opening)

Rook arrives alone, credentials visible, two grunts hanging back at the vehicles. She introduces herself as representing Helix Boundary Ltd (property management consultants). She has a notice of access for the estate agent's premises and a letter from the construction company regarding site security. Both are real documents. Neither holds up to scrutiny, but neither is obviously fake either.

Her opening position: the site is under private ownership, PORTAL has no authority here, and she'd appreciate if they wrapped up and left. She is polite. She does not threaten. She watches everything.

If the hunters leave: she lets them. She doesn't pursue. She calls in, and the site goes dark within the hour.

If the hunters push back or stall: she escalates to Phase 2.

Phase 2 — Pressure

The two grunts move up. Rook's tone doesn't change — but her body language does. She starts asking specific questions: what the hunters found, what PORTAL's interest is, whether they've made contact with ALD-1. She's building a picture. She wants to know how much PORTAL knows.

This is also when she may offer something: MESA has data on ALD-1 going back eighteen months. She can share some of it — if PORTAL shares what they found tonight. A negotiation, not a brawl. She means the offer. MESA doesn't waste leverage.

If someone takes the offer, or if the hunters back down: she gets what she wants. Play this as a quiet win for MESA that has consequences later — they now know more about PORTAL's capabilities and the team's methods.

If the hunters refuse and hold the site: she calls for the secondary team. Phase 3.

Phase 3 — Force

Two more grunts arrive. Rook steps back. The grunts are here to physically move the hunters. They will not draw weapons first — but if a hunter draws, they will respond in kind. Rook takes a position of observation: she is watching, not participating. She wants to see what the hunters can do.

This is where it can turn into something physical. Keep it fast and dirty. MESA operatives are not supernatural — but the grunts have Substance Θ available (see below) if they choose to use it, and Rook carries a field device that has a very specific effect on entities in the area.

End condition: either the hunters leave, or someone — MESA or hunter — takes enough harm that continued escalation risks a serious incident. Rook will call it off before that point unless she has a specific reason not to. She is not here to create martyrs.

MESA Field Equipment — Technical Notes

MESA's equipment parallels PORTAL's in origin but diverges sharply in application. Where PORTAL develops tools to understand liminal phenomena, MESA develops tools to contain, extract, and where necessary suppress them. The equipment below reflects approximately two years of more aggressive field research than PORTAL has conducted. The hunters may recognise the theoretical basis of some of these tools — Rex certainly will — while finding the implementation disturbing.

Substance Θ (Theta) MESA Pharmacological — Liminal Perception Compound

Function: to stabilise cognitive function in distortion zones and, at higher doses, to induce direct perception of liminal phenomena.

A proprietary compound developed by Meridian BioSciences (MESA shell company) from a research thread that began with the Hargrove Medical Centre trials. The original Meridian trials were studying anomalous neurological remission — Gordon Avery's case was one of them. The researchers noticed that some trial subjects briefly reported perceptual phenomena consistent with liminal zone exposure before their remission fully manifested. MESA extracted the active compound responsible and refined it.

Θ works by temporarily restructuring the cognitive map — the same system the Cartographer disrupts. In prophylactic doses it makes the cognitive map resistant to external disruption. In active doses it dissolves it entirely and replaces it with direct perceptual access to BIM-frequency phenomena: the user can see entities, perceive boundary effects before they manifest, and navigate by liminal topology rather than physical geography.

Prophylactic dose (what the grunts take): Resistance to displacement effects. Side effects near active BIM sources: peripheral visual artefacts, mild temporal dissociation (events feel slightly out of order), and a persistent sensation of being watched. Duration: 4–6 hours.

Active dose (what Rook takes when she chooses to): Full liminal perception for 2–3 hours. Side effects: significant temporal dissociation, emotional blunting, and a withdrawal period of 12–18 hours during which normal perception is unreliable. Long-term use degrades the cognitive map permanently — Rook is aware of this. She uses it anyway. She has a notebook she writes in during the withdrawal period. She has never shown it to anyone.

What PORTAL doesn't know: The active compound in Θ is structurally similar to the anomalous properties of BIM particulate. MESA effectively developed a drug from ash. Rex, given a sample to analyse, would recognise the BIM signature within minutes. The implications of this connect directly to Project Veil.

A dose of Substance Θ is a small grey capsule with no markings. Rook carries four. The grunts carry two each, already taken. If a hunter takes an active dose — by choice or by force — treat it as a custom move: roll +Weird. On 10+, full liminal perception for the scene, no immediate side effects. On 7–9, liminal perception and 1-harm (weird) as the cognitive map restructures. On a miss, full perceptual dissolution — the hunter cannot distinguish physical from liminal for the rest of the session without outside help.

The Anchor Spike MESA Field Device — BIM Frequency Disruptor

Function: to forcibly break contact between a person and a liminal entity, and to cause pain to entities operating at BIM frequency.

Looks like a heavy tactical pen, matte black, about 15cm. One button. One use per charge — recharging requires lab equipment. Rook carries two. The grunts do not carry them; Rook does not trust them with one-use devices in chaotic situations.

The spike emits a focused electrostatic discharge tuned to BIM frequency — the same frequency at which liminal entities operate and at which Substance Θ makes the cognitive map resonate. Against a liminal entity, it is experienced as sharp, localised pain and forces them to contract — breaking any active contact with a human and causing the entity to retreat from the immediate area. It does not destroy entities. It hurts them, in whatever way something without a nervous system experiences hurt.

Against Sven: If Rook uses the Anchor Spike on Sven, he takes 3-harm and is forced out of any supernatural state for the rest of the scene. He will also know, unambiguously, that Rook perceived what he is. She will know the same thing about him.

Against the Cartographer: If used at the epicentre during or after contact, the Spike forces the entity to contract rapidly — not to midnight-clock levels, but enough to buy an hour of reduced effect. It is not a resolution. It is a pause. And the entity will have felt it.

What PORTAL doesn't know: The Anchor Spike was designed with reference to BIM particulate as the tuning material. It is, in the most direct sense, a weapon built from the same substance that Project Veil is trying to understand. Rex examining one would recognise the theoretical framework immediately — and realise MESA is significantly ahead of PORTAL's published research timeline.

BIM Collection Array MESA Field Device — Passive Accumulator

Function: to passively collect BIM particulate from active liminal zones for later laboratory analysis.

A device about the size of a hardback book, matte grey, with a mesh face and a sealed chamber inside. It looks vaguely like a portable air purifier. The grunts carry one each in their kit bags. They have been told it is a contamination monitor. It is not. It is a vacuum-assisted accumulator that draws BIM-frequency particulate out of the ambient air in active zones.

If the hunters find one — in a kit bag, left behind, or taken from a grunt — Rex can identify what it does within a scene. The chamber contains a measurable sample of BIM particulate from the Aldermoor zone, already collected during the time the MESA team has been on-site. That sample is scientifically significant. What the hunters do with it is a decision with consequences: hand it to PORTAL for Project Veil, keep it, destroy it, or use it as leverage.

Keeper note: The BIM Collection Array is a prop with plot weight. Don't let it disappear into someone's pocket without acknowledging it. If Rex analyses the contents, that is a Project Veil breadcrumb — the sample will show that MESA has been collecting this material systematically and that the Aldermoor zone's BIM signature matches the S01 particulate from Eszter's case. Two cases. Same material. Someone has been finding it for years.

Keeper Moves — Running the Confrontation

Remember: the Keeper does not roll dice. These are the moves available to you during the confrontation. Apply them when the fiction demands it, when players look to you, or when a hunter fails a roll.

Soft Moves — Telegraph Before You Strike

  • Rook names exactly what a hunter is trying to do, quietly, correctly, without judgment.
  • A grunt's eyes slide past Sven in a way that suggests they saw something but aren't sure what.
  • Rook glances at the BIM array readout on a grunt's kit bag. Her expression doesn't change. She clicks something on her tablet.
  • One of the grunts blinks — hard — and looks at something near the epicentre that isn't there. His hand moves toward his baton.
  • Rook makes the offer. She has data. She names one specific piece of it — something the hunters don't know and clearly need. She waits.
  • The secondary vehicle arrives at the far end of the street. Two more grunts. They don't get out yet.

Hard Moves — Consequences That Land

  • A grunt takes a hunter down — not lethal, but physically restrained, out of the immediate scene.
  • Rook uses the Anchor Spike on a hunter in supernatural contact with the entity. The contact breaks. The hunter takes 1-harm (weird). Rook has what she needs from the observation.
  • Rook uses the Anchor Spike on Sven. 3-harm. She now knows what he is. He now knows she knows.
  • MESA gets the BIM array running and it collects a full sample before the hunters can stop it. MESA has what they came for. Rook calls the team back. They leave.
  • Rook produces a document — a legal notice, a property access order — that is genuine and that gives MESA standing to be here. The hunters have no legal authority. PC Adeyemi, if still present, has to make a call.
  • A grunt, peripheral-visioning hard on Substance Θ, does something the others weren't expecting. Rook's composure cracks — just slightly. She snaps an order. The moment passes. But she saw something in that grunt's face she recognises.

Aftermath Moves — After They Leave

  • MESA has the PORTAL team on camera. Rook identifies Rex Bangley by name in her debrief. CAMPBELL flags unusual activity in MESA's communication channels 48 hours later.
  • The BIM array data reaches Agatha Dell. She notes that PORTAL was present at ALD-1. She updates her model of PORTAL's research timeline. She says nothing to her operatives about what this means.
  • Rook files an internal MESA report noting that one of the PORTAL field operatives appears to be a non-biological liminal entity. She recommends reclassification of PORTAL's threat profile. This recommendation is approved.
  • If the hunters took a BIM array or an Anchor Spike, MESA knows. Rook does not escalate immediately. She notes it. She files it. She waits.
CONNECTING THIS TO FUTURE SESSIONS

Rook is not a one-session character. She is the face of MESA in the field — the person the hunters will keep meeting. She gets smarter about them every time they meet. If the hunters were cooperative tonight, she will be slightly warmer next time; if they were hostile, she will arrive prepared for that. She remembers everything.

The Substance Θ / BIM / Project Veil connection is a slow-burn revelation. Don't rush it. Let Rex carry the Anchor Spike around for a session before he gets to properly analyse it. Let the BIM array sample sit in the lab. The payoff lands harder if it arrives when the players have almost forgotten they have the evidence.

Rook's long-term trajectory: she is accumulating cognitive damage from active Θ doses. Her notebook — the one from the withdrawal periods — will eventually surface. What she writes during withdrawal is either the most important data in the campaign or the most disturbing. Possibly both.
