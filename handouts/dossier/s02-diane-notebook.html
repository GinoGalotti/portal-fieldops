Diane Marsh — Field Observations

Nine days of notes from inside the Aldermoor displacement zone. One A4 pad, blue biro, visiting nurse's handwriting.

RECOVERED: Abandoned construction site office, Aldermoor reservoir, Virginia
AUTHOR: Diane Marsh, 58, visiting nurse — 20 years serving the Aldermoor district
PERIOD: 9 days. Entries dated by tally marks, not calendar.
CONDITION: Legible throughout. Pen pressure consistent. Final pages include one sketch.
Day 1

Left for the Okafor appointment at quarter past eight. Normal route — Fenwick Road to the high street, cut through Aldermoor Gardens, ten minutes door to door. I've done it hundreds of times.

I couldn't find the high street. I was on it — I could see the post office — but every turning I took brought me back to the same stretch of Aldermoor Gardens. Walked for forty minutes before I admitted something was wrong.

Found the construction site fence. The site office was unlocked. I'm writing this inside. It's dry, there's a sleeping bag someone left, and I can see the street through the window. I'll try again in the morning.

Day 2

Tried three different routes this morning. All of them brought me back. Not to the site office — to the reservoir fence. As if every path in this part of the district drains toward the same point.

I can navigate within a small area — the site, the nearest two streets, Margaret's fence. Beyond that it stops working. It's not that I don't know where I am. I know exactly where I am. I just can't get to anywhere else. The knowing doesn't translate into movement.

Margaret left a food parcel at the fence. Bread, cheese, two apples, a note that says "Diane — stay put, love." I am staying put. I don't appear to have a choice.

// KEEPER: "The knowing doesn't translate into movement" — Diane has independently identified the mechanism. The entity consumes the functional part of familiarity while leaving the knowledge intact. CLUE: navigation works within a small radius but fails beyond it.
Day 3

Started keeping proper records. If I'm going to be here I might as well be useful.

The effect isn't constant. It's stronger at dusk and dawn — the transitions, when the light changes. During full daylight and full dark it eases slightly. Not enough to leave, but enough to walk further than I could yesterday. Midday is the clearest window.

I walked the perimeter of my navigable area at noon. Roughly three streets in each direction from the site. Beyond that, the looping starts. I've drawn a map on the back of this page.

Day 5

Margaret's food parcels arrive at 7am and 4pm. She's keeping me alive and she's doing it on a schedule. I've never been more grateful for someone else's routines.

Some streets have less of it than others. Not a gradient from the centre — it's patchy. Fenwick Road is bad. The side street where Vera lives is better. The alley behind the post office is almost clear. I don't know why. The clear patches don't correspond to distance from the construction site.

I think they correspond to how well people knew them. The alley behind the post office was a shortcut only three people used. Fenwick Road is where everyone walked. Whatever this is, it's feeding on the parts of the district that were most known.

Day 6

Saw something today that I need to write down before I decide whether I believe it.

Walking near the old reservoir at dusk — worst time, I know, but I wanted to test the perimeter during peak intensity. The street didn't look right. Not wrong exactly — older. The rowhouses on Aldermoor Gardens had front yards that haven't been there in thirty years. A phone booth on the corner of Fenwick Road. The post office sign was hand-painted.

It lasted about four minutes. Then the street was normal again and I was standing in the middle of the road with my hands shaking.

I have worked in this district for twenty years. I know what it looked like twenty years ago. What I saw was older than that. What I saw was how someone remembers this place from before I was here.

Day 7

Drew a chalk arrow on Aldermoor Gardens at the point where the loop resets. If anyone comes looking, they'll see it. I used the blue chalk from the site office supplies. It points toward the construction site because that's the direction you need to go to find me and it's the direction the street won't let you go.

I've started leaving markers on my navigable routes — chalk ticks at the turnings where the looping begins. Five so far. They stay where I put them. The effect doesn't erase physical marks. It just makes you unable to follow them.

The chalk stays. The knowing doesn't. Whatever this is, it works on minds, not on the street itself.

Day 8

The excavation at the reservoir site. I've been avoiding it — it's fenced off properly and I didn't see the point. Today I looked.

They dug about four metres down before they stopped. The substrate is clay and gravel, then something darker underneath — not soil, something geological. The dig walls are still clean. Nobody has been down there since the work stopped.

There is something at the bottom. I don't mean I saw something. I mean I stood at the edge and felt the same quality of attention I feel on the streets — the sense of being noticed — but concentrated. Focused. Coming from below.

I left. I'm not going back to the edge.

// KEEPER: CLUE: the entity's presence is concentrated at the excavation — "coming from below." The construction disturbed something that was dormant. Diane won't go back, which is the correct instinct.
Day 9

I went back. I know I said I wouldn't. I went at dawn, which is the worst time, because I wanted to see it at full intensity.

The substrate layer of the excavation — the dark geological material — had something in it. Not an object. A shape. A door-shaped shadow in the rock. Not a door, but the memory of one. As if something had pressed against the stone from the other side, a very long time ago, and left an impression.

I drew it. I don't know what it means. I drew it because I've been here nine days and I've run out of explanations that make sense and this is what's left.

Day 9 — evening

Someone will come. I don't know when, but the food parcels are still arriving and Margaret is still leaving her light on, which means people outside know I'm here and haven't given up.

If you're reading this and you've come to help: the midday window is your best approach. Stick to the alley behind the post office — it's the clearest route. Follow my chalk marks. Don't look too hard at the streets during dusk — the visual contradiction makes it worse.

I have nine days of data. Tell me what you need and I'll tell you what I know.
