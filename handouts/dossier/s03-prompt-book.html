The Prompt Book — Artefact Analysis

19th-century theatrical prompt book. Acquired via Solstice Property Group grant. BIM-saturated.

LOCATION: Foyer display case (case 3), Meridian Theatre Company, Fredericksburg, VA
ACQUISITION: Private estate auction, $4,200. Grant-funded by Solstice Property Group.
DATE OF ACQUISITION: 20 months ago
PROVENANCE: Private collection, acquired 1887. Originally owned by a Victorian stage performer (name unrecorded in acquisition documents).
PHYSICAL: Leather-bound, 8×10", approximately 200 pages. Annotations throughout in a precise, consistent hand. Condition: good. Binding intact. Pages show age-appropriate foxing.
BIM READING: 1.72 — highest reading in the building. Five times the Aldermoor zone edge.
Physical Examination

Standard Victorian prompt book format — printed play text with wide margins for annotation. The play is The Tempest, not Two Gentlemen. The book's content is irrelevant to the mechanism. What matters is the annotations and what they accumulated over decades of use.

The binding is professional — rebound at least once, probably in the 1920s based on the leather treatment. The pages are original. The pen pressure in the annotations increases progressively through the book. Early pages: light, precise. Later pages: heavier, as if the writer was pressing harder to make the ink hold.

The book is heavier than it should be for its size. Not dramatically — maybe 15% more than a comparable volume. Clara Voss noted this in her first notebook entry. She was right to notice it.

Annotations — First Half (pp. 1–100)

The early annotations are conventional stage directions. Blocking, entrances, exits, timing marks. The hand is precise and unhurried — someone who has done this many times.

Page 12 — Act I, Scene II
Prospero enters from stage right. Three beats before speaking. The audience must feel his weight before they hear his voice. He is the island before he is the man.
Page 34 — Act II, Scene I
Ariel's entrance: from above if the rigging permits. The spirit should arrive from a direction the audience was not watching. Misdirection is consent — they choose to look away, and the arrival rewards the looking back.

These are good notes. They're the work of someone who understood performance deeply and thought carefully about the relationship between performer, space, and audience. Nothing unusual about them except their quality.

// KEEPER: The early annotations are genuinely just excellent stage directions. They establish the author as a serious practitioner. Players may assume these are the clue — they're not. The shift comes later.
Annotations — Middle Section (pp. 100–150)

Around page 100, the annotations change. Not abruptly — the shift is gradual enough that you could miss it if you were reading quickly. The handwriting is the same. The ink is the same. But the annotations stop being instructions for a performance and start being instructions for something else.

Page 108 — Act III, Scene III
The performer must not resist the arrival. The character comes when the performer stops holding the boundary. This is not a failure of technique. It is the technique's completion.
Page 127 — Act IV, Scene I
The audience is not watching. The audience is participating. Their belief is the medium through which the transfer occurs. Without them, the performer is alone with the character. With them, the character has somewhere to arrive from.

These aren't stage directions. They're instructions for the mechanism — how to lower the boundary between performer and role. Whoever wrote this understood what was happening and was writing a manual for it.

Annotations — Final Section (pp. 150–200)

The final fifty pages are in the same hand but not quite the same voice. The annotations become less instructional and more observational — as if the writer has stopped teaching the technique and started documenting what happens when it succeeds.

Page 168 — Act V, Scene I
He is here now. I can feel him in the way the lines arrive — not from memory but from somewhere older. Prospero has been in this building longer than I have. He was here before the first audience sat down. I am the latest in a series of vessels and I am, I think, the most willing.
Page 191 — Final page with annotations
Tonight the house was full. I could feel every one of them believing. The transfer was complete before the interval. I have not been alone onstage in eleven years and I do not expect to be alone again. This is not a complaint. This is the finest work I have ever done.

The Victorian performer knew what was happening to him. He wasn't a victim — he was a practitioner who documented his own displacement. When he died onstage, he was fully inside the role. The death left a mark on the book: the accumulated charge of decades of audience belief, focused through one man's complete surrender to the mechanism. That's what we're reading at 1.72 BIM.
